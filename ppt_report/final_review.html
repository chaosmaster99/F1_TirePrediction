Introduction

The Critical Role of Tyres in F1 Performance

Tyres are the sole contact point between the car and the track, making them absolutely crucial for achieving optimal grip, speed, and overall performance in Formula 1 racing.


Tyre degradation significantly impacts lap times and race pace, forcing teams to make critical strategic decisions. How a tyre wears down can determine whether a driver gains or loses positions.


Modern F1 races are a delicate balance of managing tyre wear, optimizing their lifespan, and executing strategically timed pit stops. These elements collectively dictate the outcome of a Grand Prix.

Why This Matters

  • A poorly timed pit stop can cost 20–30 seconds
  • Tyre degradation is non-linear and non-stationary
  • Degradation depends on compound, circuit, driver style, fuel load, and track temperature
  • Teams currently rely on heuristic models and engineer intuition
  • Data-driven approaches can provide quantifiable decision support
04
Problem Statement

Formula 1 Pit Stop Timing Optimization

Tyre degradation plays a critical role in determining race performance and pit stop strategy in Formula 1. The condition of a tyre changes dynamically over time due to multiple factors such as:

Track Temperature & Surface
Fuel Load Reduction
Driving Style & Aggression
Tyre Compound Selection

This degradation behavior is non-stationary, meaning its statistical properties change across race laps and stints.

Research Question:
Can we accurately model and forecast this non-stationary tyre degradation using time series analysis to enable optimal pit stop and race strategy decisions?
05
Research Gaps

Identified Gaps in Existing Literature

1. Fragmented Approaches

Existing studies address tyre degradation prediction, uncertainty-aware modelling, and pit stop strategy optimization as separate problems rather than as a unified system.

2. No Decision Integration

Predictive models achieve high accuracy but are not integrated with real-time decision-making algorithms for pit stop strategy.

3. Simplified Assumptions

Strategy optimization approaches rely on simplified or heuristic tyre degradation assumptions, limiting their effectiveness under dynamic race conditions.

4. No Interactive Tool

Most solutions are offline or research-focused, with no simple, interactive user interface for simulation, testing, or real-time strategy evaluation.

06
Research Objective

Unified Research Objective

To develop an end-to-end predictive system that models non-stationary tyre degradation using multiple time series and machine learning approaches, compares their effectiveness, and integrates the best-performing model into an interactive pit stop strategy engine for real-time race decision support.

Literature Review

Summary of Literature Review

  • Most existing works focus on individual aspects — either degradation prediction OR strategy optimization — but not both in an integrated pipeline
  • Statistical time series methods (ARIMA, VAR) are well-established for forecasting but have not been systematically applied to tyre degradation at the stint level
  • Machine learning models (XGBoost, LSTM) capture complex non-linear interactions but are rarely compared against traditional TS models on the same F1 data
  • No existing work provides a dual-level evaluation framework (instance-level vs. curve-level) that distinguishes between per-lap accuracy and whole-stint forecasting
  • There is a clear gap in interactive, deployable tools — most research produces offline analyses without a user-facing application

Our Contribution

This project bridges the gap by building an end-to-end system that trains, compares, and deploys multiple time series models into a unified strategy engine with an interactive web interface — integrating prediction and decision-making into one pipeline.

Data Collection

Data Fetching — FastF1 API Pipeline

Why FastF1?

FastF1 is a Python library that wraps the official F1 live-timing API. It provides lap-by-lap timing, telemetry, tyre compound, sector splits, and positional data — everything needed for degradation modelling.

  • Official source — same data F1 teams see on the pit wall
  • Lap-level granularity (not just race results)
  • Tyre compound + stint information per lap
  • Speed-trap and sector timing for performance context

Why 2023 & 2024?

  • Ground-effect regulations stabilised after 2022 overhaul
  • Same tyre supplier (Pirelli) & compound numbering
  • 24 races across both years — enough diversity in circuits & weather
  • Recent data = most relevant for current predictions

Fetching Pipeline (3 Stages)

1

Session Loading

For each Grand Prix, load the Race session via fastf1.get_session(year, gp, 'R'). This downloads all timing data from the F1 servers.

2

Lap Extraction

Call session.laps to get every lap from every driver. Extract 24 raw features including LapTime, Compound, TyreLife, SpeedI1/I2/FL/ST, sector times, and position.

3

Metadata Tagging

Annotate each lap with Driver, Team, Circuit, Year, and TotalLaps. Output saved as f1_lap_data.csv.

Caching

FastF1 caches raw API responses locally in cache/. Subsequent runs skip network calls — builds are reproducible and fast (< 5 s for cached data).

5 Categories of Noise Removal

#FilterWhat It RemovesWhy It Matters
1 Lap-time window Laps < 50 s or > 200 s Eliminates pit-in/out laps, red-flag laps, and timing glitches
2 WET / INTER removal Laps on wet-weather tyres Project scope is dry compounds only (SOFT, MEDIUM, HARD)
3 Safety-car filter Laps where TrackStatus ≠ normal racing SC/VSC artificially slows pace; pollutes degradation signal
4 NaN drop Rows with missing LapTime or sectors Ensures every row has a valid timing measurement
5 IQR outlier clip Lap times beyond 1.5 × IQR per circuit Removes extreme values that skew regression targets

Why This Matters for Modelling

Every removed lap is non-representative of normal racing degradation. Keeping them would teach models that “lap time can randomly jump 30 seconds” — wrecking both the regression target (FuelCorrectedLapTime) and the degradation curves used by ARIMA/VAR. Cleaning is not optional; it is the foundation of signal quality.

Model Architecture

ARIMA — Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

Raw Lap Times
Per compound (SOFT / MED / HARD)
↓
Average Degradation Curve
Mean ΔLapTime vs TyreLife across all stints
↓
Stationarity Tests
ADF & KPSS → determine differencing order (d)
Order Selection
AIC grid search → best (p, d, q)
↓
ARIMA(p, d, q) Fit
Typically (1,1,0) or (1,2,0)
↓
One Predicted Degradation Curve per Compound
Curve-level evaluation (RMSE, MAE, R²)

How It Works

  • AR (p) — uses past degradation values to predict next
  • I (d) — differences the series to achieve stationarity
  • MA (q) — incorporates past forecast errors
  • Fitted separately for each tyre compound

Limitations

  • Produces one curve per compound — cannot distinguish drivers or circuits
  • Univariate — ignores cross-compound dynamics
  • Serves as a baseline for comparison

VAR — Vector Autoregression

3 Averaged Degradation Curves
SOFT + MEDIUM + HARD (coupled system)
↓
Lag Selection
AIC → optimal lag order (LAG 1)
Granger Causality Test
Checks if one compound predicts another
↓
VAR(1) Model
Each compound = f(own lag, other compound lags)
↓
Multivariate Forecast
3 simultaneous degradation curve predictions

How It Works

  • Models all 3 compounds simultaneously as a vector system
  • Captures cross-compound dynamics — SOFT degradation leads MEDIUM & HARD
  • Impulse response shows how a shock in one compound propagates to others

Key Advantage over ARIMA

  • Multivariate — captures lead-lag relationships ARIMA misses
  • SOFT Granger-causes MEDIUM (p=0.002) and HARD (p=0.009)
  • 16% RMSE improvement over ARIMA at curve level

LSTM — Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory

Sliding Window (8 laps × 9 features)
TyreLife, Compound, Stint, Fuel, Speed, Circuit, Driver…
↓
LSTM (32 units)
Recurrent Dropout 0.1 + BatchNorm
↓
Dense(16, ReLU) → Dropout(0.1)
↓
Dense(1) — Predicted ΔLapTime
Huber loss, Adam lr=0.0003, 120 epochs

How It Works

  • Sequence model — learns temporal patterns from 8-lap windows
  • Simplified architecture — single LSTM layer avoids overfitting on small datasets
  • Autoregressive mode — iteratively predicts future laps for strategy

Performance & Tradeoffs

  • Simplified single-layer design achieves R² = 0.110 — positive and meaningful
  • Reduced regularisation lets the model learn more signal from ~22K samples
  • Still limited by data scale; needs more seasons to match tree-based models

How It Works

  • Ensemble of decision trees — each tree corrects the errors of the previous one
  • Captures non-linear interactions (compound × circuit × tyre-life) automatically via tree splits
  • No sequence assumption — treats each lap independently
  • Feature importance reveals TyreLife and CompoundEncoded as dominant predictors

Why It Excels Here

  • Best instance-level R² = 0.448 across all models
  • Rich feature space gives it more signal than curve-only models
  • Dual head: regression (degradation) + classification (pit decision)
  • Tuned classifier: Precision 63.1% > Recall 53.6% (avoids false pit calls)

↓

How It Works

  • Stage 1: ARIMA provides a per-compound base prediction (weak signal, R² ≈ 0.006)

Models Compared

Finding 1: XGBoost Dominates Instance-Level

XGBoost captures ~45% of explainable variance in per-lap degradation after removing data leakage (leaked features inflated earlier R² to 0.98). This honest R²=0.448 remains the strongest instance-level result, with the Hybrid (R²=0.441) closely matching it via residual stacking.

Finding 2: VAR Outperforms Univariate TS at Curve Level

VAR outperforms ARIMA at curve-level (RMSE 0.850 vs 1.018) by modelling cross-compound dynamics as a multivariate system. Capturing lead-lag relationships between SOFT, MEDIUM, and HARD degradation gives VAR a 16% RMSE improvement over univariate ARIMA.

Finding 3: Hybrid Validates Residual Stacking

The redesigned Hybrid (ARIMA base + XGBoost residual) achieves R²=0.441 — nearly matching standalone XGBoost (0.448). ARIMA contributes a weak base signal (R²≈0.006); XGBoost corrects compound/circuit/driver residuals. Key insight: residual stacking correctly combines time-series structure with ML, unlike the previous alpha-blend which conflated evaluation paradigms.

Finding 4: LSTM Simplified Architecture Shows Positive R²

After simplifying to a single LSTM(32) layer with reduced regularisation (Dropout 0.15, recurrent_dropout 0.1, Huber loss, lr=0.0003, 120 epochs), the LSTM achieves R²=0.110 at instance level — a meaningful positive result. The lighter architecture avoids overfitting on ~22K samples. While it does not match XGBoost (0.448), its autoregressive curve forecasts remain smooth and plausible. Further gains require more seasons or richer temporal features.

Conclusions

Conclusions

1

XGBoost is the optimal model for pit stop prediction — achieving R² = 0.448 and F1 = 0.580 for pit stop classification after rigorous data-leakage removal, it is the strongest instance-level model using a clean 9-feature vector spanning compound, circuit, driver, and fuel effects.

2

Dual-level evaluation is essential — Instance-level and curve-level metrics measure fundamentally different things. XGBoost dominates instance-level; VAR performs best at curve-level. Reporting only one paradigm would misrepresent model capabilities.

3

Multivariate time series outperforms univariate for curve forecasting — VAR beats ARIMA by 16% in RMSE by modelling compound interactions jointly, demonstrating that cross-compound dynamics carry predictive signal beyond what univariate models capture.

4

The end-to-end system demonstrates practical value — By integrating prediction into an interactive strategy engine, the system bridges the gap between academic degradation modeling and actionable race strategy decisions.

5

Residual stacking is the correct ensemble approach — The ARIMA + XGBoost residual hybrid (R²=0.441) validates that combining a time-series base with ML residual correction works, while naive alpha-blending of curves fails. Matching model paradigms to evaluation level is critical.

  • Driver-specific and circuit-specific strategy recommendations
  • Full end-to-end pipeline: data fetch → train → evaluate → deploy

Limitations

  • No weather data — wet/intermediate conditions excluded; track temperature not available in FastF1 lap data
  • No real-time telemetry — model uses historical timing data, not live car sensors
  • Limited seasons — 2 years may not capture regulation changes or long-term compound evolution
  • Safety car / red flag events — these disrupt degradation patterns; partially filtered but not fully modeled
  • LSTM data constraints — ~22K samples with seq_len=8 yields R²=0.110 but remains below tree-based models
  • Static driver profiles — profiles are epoch-level averages, not adaptive to within-race evolution

Future Work

  • Incorporate 2025 season data and regulation changes
  • Add track temperature from weather APIs
  • Residual correction hybrid (ARIMA on XGBoost residuals)
  • Transformer-based architecture for longer sequences
  • Real-time telemetry integration via live timing feed
  • Probabilistic predictions with uncertainty quantification
